Part 1

The Basics, in Plain Language

Your DNA is an instruction manual, but it never leaves the nucleus of the cell. To build something from those instructions, the cell first makes a working copy of one gene at a time — that copy is called mRNA. The mRNA copy then travels to a molecular machine called a ribosome, which reads it and builds a protein from it.

DNA → mRNA → ribosome → protein. This is the basic flow every step of this course sits on top of.
Source: Wikimedia Commons, "Central dogma of molecular biology.svg" (CC BY-SA 3.0).

What a ribosome actually does

Think of the ribosome as a construction worker reading instructions three letters at a time. Each group of three letters — a codon — tells it exactly which building block to add next. The building blocks are amino acids, and a chain of them strung together — before it's finished and folded into its working 3D shape — is called a polypeptide. "Polypeptide" just means "a protein under construction." Once it's fully built and folded, we usually just call it "the protein."

The ribosome moves along the mRNA one codon at a time, adding one amino acid per codon, until it reaches a stop codon (shown in red).
Source: Wikimedia Commons, "Ribosome mRNA translation en.svg" by LadyofHats (public domain).

The stop codon, and what "stop_gained" breaks

Every gene has a designated codon near the very end that means "stop building — you're done." A stop_gained mutation — the type of variant found in this case — is a DNA typo that accidentally creates one of these "stop" signals somewhere in the middle of the gene, far too early. The ribosome obeys it anyway; it has no way to know the signal is a mistake. The result is a short, broken fragment instead of the full protein.

A stop_gained mutation inserts a premature "STOP" signal partway through the gene, truncating the protein.
Source: Wikimedia Commons, "Amino Acid Codon Table.svg" (CC BY-SA 4.0). UAA, UAG, and UGA are the three stop codons — the ones a stop_gained mutation creates in the wrong place.

NMD (nonsense-mediated decay) — the cell's quality control

The cell doesn't just tolerate broken instructions sitting around. A built-in proofreading system called NMD scans mRNA copies for stop signals appearing in the wrong place. When it finds one, it usually destroys that mRNA copy entirely — before it can even be used by a ribosome many times over. So the real-world effect of a stop_gained mutation usually isn't "a slightly short protein floating around." It's closer to: that copy of the instructions gets shredded before it's used, so almost no protein comes from that gene copy at all.

NMD is quality control at the mRNA level — it destroys transcripts with premature stop codons before they're used.

Two databases worth demystifying

ClinVar is a public, shared notebook where laboratories report "we found this exact DNA variant in a patient, and here's whether we believe it causes disease." If a variant is logged as "pathogenic," someone has seen this exact typo before and already built a case that it's harmful.

gnomAD is a database recording how common each variant is across roughly 800,000 sequenced people. If a variant shows up in a large fraction of ordinary, healthy people, it's almost certainly harmless — a disease this rare cannot be explained by a change that 1 in 20 people casually carry without being sick.

The drug idea, in one sentence

Ataluren is a pill that makes the ribosome slightly "sloppy" right at one specific broken stop signal — just enough that it occasionally skips past it, inserts some amino acid instead of stopping, and keeps building all the way to the real, correct stop point at the true end of the protein. It doesn't touch the DNA — it nudges the reading machine to push through the fake stop sign some fraction of the time.

Ataluren lowers ribosome fidelity at the mistaken stop codon, letting translation continue through to the true end of the gene some of the time.

Part 2

The Deeper Mechanism (for the writeup)

2.1 What "stop_gained" does at the ribosome, precisely

Translation is terminated by two proteins, eRF1 and eRF3, which recognize a stop codon sitting in the ribosome's "A site" and trigger release of the polypeptide being built. When a stop_gained mutation creates an abnormally early stop codon, NMD typically recognizes it — the general rule of thumb is a stop codon located more than ~50 nucleotides before the last exon–exon junction — and marks that transcript for degradation. Net effect: not "a slightly shorter working protein," but usually "little or no protein at all," because the mRNA is cleared before repeated translation, and whatever truncated fragment is made is typically non-functional and often degraded further.

This case The variant 15:40209701 T>G is stop-gained, already ClinVar pathogenic/likely pathogenic for BUB1B-related MVA — a strong loss-of-function allele, not a partial-function one.

2.2 The therapeutic idea: ribosomal misreading

A stop codon is, chemically, just a normal-length codon that happens to have no matching tRNA (the molecule that delivers amino acids) — so instead of an amino acid being added, eRF1/eRF3 step in and terminate. Readthrough drugs lower the ribosome's fidelity at that moment, allowing a near-cognate tRNA (one that almost, but not perfectly, matches the stop codon) to be inserted instead of triggering termination. The ribosome "reads through" the mistaken stop signal, inserts some amino acid, and continues to the gene's real stop codon.

  • The rescued protein isn't identical to wild-type — one amino acid near the mutation site differs from the original. Whether the protein still works depends on how tolerant that exact position is to substitution (the same kind of reasoning SIFT/PolyPhen apply to missense variants, just applied here to the readthrough substitution).
  • Readthrough is never 100% efficient. Some fraction of translation events succeed; the rest still terminate early. This is a partial rescue therapy.
  • Not all stop codons are equally "leaky." UGA is empirically easiest for near-cognate tRNAs to misread, UAG is intermediate, UAA is hardest. Worth confirming which stop codon T>G creates here.
  • Sequence context around the stop codon matters too (particularly the nucleotide immediately after it), independent of which stop codon it is.

2.3 Ataluren specifically

  • Approved status: Ataluren (Translarna, originally PTC124) is approved in the EU/EEA and other jurisdictions for nonsense-mutation Duchenne muscular dystrophy (patients ≥2 years). A cystic fibrosis Phase III trial was discontinued for lack of significant benefit — a real, imperfect, context-dependent drug, not a universal fix.
  • Mechanism, precisely: an orally bioavailable small molecule (an oxadiazole) that promotes readthrough specifically at premature stop codons while largely preserving correct termination at normal, full-length stop codons.
  • Track record beyond DMD: tested with varying success in cystic fibrosis, some lysosomal storage diseases (e.g. Hurler syndrome/MPS I-H), aniridia, and case reports in other single-gene conditions — a "class of precedent" argument, not proof it will work for BUB1B.
Part 3

Applying This to the BUB1B Finding

  1. Where does the truncation fall relative to functional domains? BUB1B/BUBR1 has an N-terminal kinetochore-binding region, a central GLEBS domain (binds BUB3, essential for checkpoint signaling), and a C-terminal pseudokinase domain. If position 40,209,701 truncates the protein before domains required for checkpoint function, restoring full-length translation matters a great deal — worth mapping against the MANE Select transcript coordinates from Track 1.
  2. Does this allele get targeted by NMD, or partly escape it? Readthrough drugs act on transcripts that reach the ribosome — if the mRNA is destroyed first, there's nothing to rescue.
  3. Which stop codon does T>G create, and what's the surrounding sequence? Determines expected baseline readthrough efficiency before any drug is applied.
  4. The compound-het partner is a genuinely favorable feature. The second BUB1B allele carries a missense variant, not a second nonsense mutation — readthrough therapy would only need to rescue one allele to potentially restore meaningful checkpoint function.
Claim to make "Ataluren represents a mechanistically plausible, already-approved candidate for partial restoration of BUB1B function, specifically because the causal variant is a premature stop codon — the exact mutation class this drug class was designed for — and because only one of the two alleles needs partial rescue given the compound-heterozygous genotype."
Claim to avoid "Ataluren will treat this child's MVA." There's no efficacy data yet for this gene, this variant, or this specific stop codon identity — stating that plainly is a rigor point in your favor, not a weakness.

Concrete next step to name (even without time to execute it): an in vitro minigene readthrough reporter assay — clone the genomic region spanning the mutation into a reporter construct, treat cells with ataluren, and measure whether full-length protein reappears by western blot. This is the standard first validation step used across the literature before any clinical extrapolation.
